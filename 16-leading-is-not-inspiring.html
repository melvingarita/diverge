Leadership is sharpening the thinking of others.

Leading Is Not Inspiring

Inspiration mobilizes an organization, but it does not prove the decision is right. In banking, where mistakes take time to surface, leadership demands something else: conviction that comes after challenge, not before.

NOV 2026 · 6 MIN READ · BY MELVIN GARITA ES EN

In many committees, there is a moment that carries more weight than the vote itself: the instant the most senior person in the room speaks. From then on, the conversation changes character. The question is no longer whether the assumption holds, whether the pricing compensates for the risk, or whether the adverse scenario is plausible. What gets calculated, silently, is the cost of disagreeing.

None of it makes it into the minutes.

The decisions that define an organization rarely arrive with conclusive evidence. They arrive under pressure: targets to hit, a market watching, teams waiting for a signal. Under that pressure, the organization needs direction, and the temptation is to mistake direction for conviction.

What does it mean to lead, beyond inspiring? And why, in banking, does that question carry a higher price?

Buy-In as a Credential

It would be easy to answer with a caricature: that leaders merely inspire, and that inspiration is just rhetoric.

The evidence does not allow it. Judge and Piccolo’s meta-analysis, drawing on 87 sources, found a consistent relationship between transformational leadership and several measures of effectiveness, including motivation, satisfaction, and performance. Motivation mobilizes effort and makes a decision easier to execute.

But that evidence does not license the conclusion that a decision is sound because the team stands behind it. Antonakis and House showed that leadership also requires instrumental functions, such as reading the environment, setting objectives, and correcting course, and that leaving them out leads to overstating the effect of transformational leadership.

Even so, management practice has turned the ability to generate buy-in into the most visible proof of leadership. Executives are judged by the climate on their teams, by the alignment they achieve, by the energy with which they communicate strategy. These are signals of how the organization feels about a decision, not of whether the decision is right.

In banking, that gap becomes an exposure. A bank operates with leverage, funded by depositors and creditors, and deterioration in its loan book can take time to surface.

In that context, many prudent decisions are the ones that generate the least enthusiasm: slowing down a profitable business line, turning away an attractive client, recognizing a loss before it becomes obvious.

Buy-in is a condition for execution.

It is not evidence of decision quality.

What Conviction Does to Information

The risk is not that the leader inspires, but what visible conviction can do to the flow of information. It is reasonable to expect a persuasive leader to lower the cost of agreeing and, without intending to, raise the cost of dissent.

When that happens, bad news arrives sugarcoated, adverse scenarios are framed as unlikely before they are discussed, and models begin to justify the preferred decision rather than stress-test it. The absence of objections is read as validation.

Small financial markets add a second mechanism, distinct from the effect of hierarchy. The same executives cross paths for decades on boards, in industry associations, and on committees. Today’s dissenter will be tomorrow’s peer, counterparty, or candidate. In those markets, the cost of disagreeing does not end when the meeting adjourns: it compounds over a career.

Supervisors have seen the symptom. In 2014, the Financial Stability Board identified effective communication and challenge as one of the indicators of a sound risk culture.

In 2023, ECB Banking Supervision noted that banks need to improve the capacity to challenge board members on their decisions, and that some risk and compliance functions were not challenging the business enough or were being overruled by it.

Those findings suggest that the problem is not merely one of formal channels, but of stature, incentives, and consequences.

An organization can hear every objection and still let none of them carry weight.

The Price of the Comfortable Decision

The cost of that dynamic does not show up in the employee engagement survey. It shows up when the comfortable decision starts to come due.

The Federal Reserve’s review of Silicon Valley Bank, published in April 2023, concluded that the board and senior management failed to manage the bank’s risks adequately, and it also acknowledged shortcomings in supervision itself.

It further found that the board did not receive adequate information about those risks, that the bank repeatedly failed its own internal liquidity stress tests, and that management shifted to less conservative assumptions that masked part of the risk.

The case does not show how deliberations unfolded, but it illustrates what happens when models are adjusted to fit the decision instead of testing it.

There is a second, less obvious factor. In 2022, Dorison, Umphres, and Lerner asked how much trust someone who persists with a failing course of action earns, and whether that trust is deserved.

They studied the question in three preregistered experiments, with 3,888 participants recruited online in the United States. All three compared those who persisted with those who changed course.

  • In the first, those who persisted were perceived as having more integrity and benevolence.
  • In the second, in a trust game, observers entrusted them with 29% more money.
  • In the third, using the same type of game, they returned 15% more: they were, in fact, more trustworthy.

This is not banking evidence. But it helps explain why persistence may not register as a warning sign.

That calls for a distinction. Standing by a difficult decision is a virtue as long as the evidence still supports it. Persisting to protect one’s reputation or the consistency of one’s narrative is escalation of commitment with good press.

“

When a decision can only be sustained if the evidence is bent to fit it (by softening scenarios, tweaking assumptions, or sidelining whoever brings bad news), there is no longer any leadership to defend. There is a preference shielded by hierarchy.

Conviction After Challenge

To lead, then, is not to get others to follow a decision. It is to help others examine it more rigorously before they follow it: to make assumptions explicit, stress-test scenarios, and define what evidence would change it. Inspiring and challenging are not at odds: they come in sequence.

The proposal rests on three practices.

  1. Sequencing. The most senior person speaks last, a well-established committee practice, and each predefined criterion is assessed independently before reaching an overall judgment, as Kahneman, Lovallo, and Sibony proposed. Sequencing dampens the effect of hierarchy; it does not eliminate the relational cost of dissent.
  2. The review trigger. It addresses that relational cost. Every material decision is approved together with the evidence that would require it to be revisited, and someone is tasked with looking for that evidence. That way, whoever brings it forward is not challenging anyone: they are fulfilling an agreed condition.
  3. The explicit trade-off. The leader names what is being given up before the organization discovers it on its own.

The cost is real: slower decisions and an openness that some will read as doubt.

In a bank, one diagnostic signal is the share of credit and risk decisions in which a documented disagreement changed the pricing, the limit, the tenor, or the terms. It should not become a target, because it would incentivize box-ticking objections, nor a scorecard for the committee.

It works as an alarm: if it sits at zero for months, it should trigger a case review. Was there dissent? How was it resolved?

Inspiring mobilizes the organization. Leading is creating the conditions for the decision to deserve that effort.
