Phase V: The Invisible Infrastructure · Article 14

Culture, control, and governance of a complex institution.

Internal Fragmentation as a Governance Failure

A flawless org chart can be the best hiding place for risk.

NOV 2026 · 6 MIN READ · BY MELVIN GARITA ES EN

Every growing bank specializes. It builds business units, subsidiaries, control functions, committees, and support areas, each with its own mandate, its own metrics, and its own language. Specialization is not a flaw. It is the sensible way to manage complexity.

The problem begins when complexity grows faster than the capacity to integrate it. Material decisions stop living within a single unit and start living between units: between the business and risk, between the subsidiary and the parent, between technology and operations.

At that point, integration is no longer automatic. It no longer happens because people know each other or because everything reaches the same table. It has to be designed, assigned, and enforced.

Yet defining mandates usually gets more attention than governing the interdependencies between them. The bank knows what each unit must do. It does not always know who resolves what no unit can decide alone.

Fragmentation is not an org-chart problem. It is evidence that no one governs the interdependencies.

Reorganization as a Reflex

Faced with fragmentation, the usual response is structural: redraw the org chart, merge areas, create an integration unit, or add another committee.

The logic seems sound. If the pieces don't fit, redraw the map. A reorganization also offers something boards value: a visible action, with an announcement date and named owners. Real governance, by contrast, rarely makes for a press release.

The evidence counsels caution. In their classic 1967 study, Lawrence and Lorsch found that the highest-performing firms in their sample combined differentiation with integration: specialization required mechanisms to integrate.

The UK supervisors' review of the failure of HBOS, published in 2015, documented failures of strategy, of governance, and of board challenge to management. Read through the lens of integration, the case suggests that formal structure is not enough when strategy and oversight do not operate across the institution as a whole.

The org chart allocates responsibilities. It does not ensure that anyone is accountable for the whole.

A reorganization may temporarily improve coordination without addressing the absence of governance over the new boundaries. Over time, the new units tend to develop their own metrics and incentives. The boundaries move. The governance gap gets a new org chart.

The mistake is treating structural redesign as a substitute for governance.

When Every Unit Delivers and the Bank Does Not

There is a risk that fragmentation conceals with particular effectiveness: every unit meets its targets while the institution accumulates an exposure that no one owns.

The scene is familiar: every area reports green. The business is growing, risk is operating within limits, compliance has no findings. The open question is who is watching what happens between those reports.

A collection of green reports does not add up to a green institution.

The Basel Committee made this point in its 2015 corporate governance principles for banks: banks should avoid organizational silos that impede the sharing of risk information, and the parent should be aware of the material risks that may affect the group and its subsidiaries.

The Archegos case shows the cost of that gap. The independent review commissioned by Credit Suisse documented identified risks, persistent limit breaches, and failures to act and escalate between the business and control functions. The loss reached roughly US$5.5 billion. The warning signs were known. The response failed.

In Costa Rica, the Regulation on Consolidated Supervision (CONASSIF Resolution 16-22) addresses the same concern: Articles 55 and 56 are devoted to integrated risk management at the group or conglomerate level and to the concentration of significant risks.

Fragmented risk does not disappear. The bank bears it, even if no unit acknowledges it as its own.

The Cost No One Attributes

The cost of fragmentation is rarely identified as such: it is scattered across expenses, losses, delays, and missed opportunities. Each area can explain its share. The bank pays the total.

It shows up in decisions that optimize local results at the expense of institutional ones: lending that consumes capital without comparing its value against alternative uses; transactions that each meet individual approval criteria yet build up an exposure the institution never assesses on an integrated basis; duplicated technology because no forum prioritizes competing demands.

It also shows up in management time: issues escalated again and again without resolution, because no one brings together the information and the authority to close them. Escalation moves the issue; it does not resolve it.

And it worsens when incentives reward turf. If every manager is assessed only on what they control, they have little reason to invest in what they share. The organization begins to resemble the taifa kingdoms of medieval Iberia: each unit with its own court, its own territory, and its own metrics; none accountable for the whole.

Collaboration is left to goodwill, and goodwill is not a governance mechanism.

The usual reaction is to add another committee, as if what were missing were meetings rather than authority. But a committee without decision rights, without consolidated information, and without follow-up does not integrate: it merely convenes.

No material risk should be left without someone mandated to integrate it, or without an escalation path that reaches whoever has the authority to decide.

Once that line is crossed, even if every unit fulfills its mandate, fragmentation is no longer an efficiency problem. It becomes a corporate governance failure.

Governing Interdependencies

The answer is neither to centralize everything nor to reorganize yet again. Assigning accountability for each outcome is not enough. The bank must govern the decisions that no unit can resolve on its own.

The 2023 G20/OECD Principles of Corporate Governance place oversight of risk management with the board. It is the board's role to demand integration and to verify that it works. Senior management identifies the material interdependencies and designates owners to integrate decisions, with defined information and decision rights. Control functions retain their independence; business units, their obligations.

The integration owner risks becoming yet another bureaucratic layer. The role must not duplicate approvals: it gathers information, frames a proposal, and escalates it, within a defined timeframe, to the competent authority. It integrates across units; it does not sign off above all of them.

The cost is explicit: some units will have to accept decisions that do not maximize their local results. Incentives will need to recognize institutional outcomes, and certain decisions will require more deliberation. Resistance will come with legitimate arguments: agility, specialization, clarity of mandates.

The test is not how many owners are appointed, but how long cross-unit decisions take to resolve and how many are executed on time. If the same issue keeps coming back unresolved or unexecuted, the mechanism must be reviewed, not shielded by another committee.

Structure allocates responsibilities. Governance answers for what happens between them.
