Transparency and Alignment as a Competitive Advantage

A bank does not need to publish everything it decides. It needs to be able to explain it. The transparency that creates advantage does not begin with disclosure: it begins with how each decision is built.

NOV 2026 · 6 MIN READ · BY MELVIN GARITA ES EN

Every mature institution knows how to approve decisions. It has competent bodies, delegated authorities, minutes and files. Far less common is the ability, months later, to explain a decision as clearly as it was approved: which version of the data was used, which assumptions drove the outcome, which alternatives were ruled out and which risk was knowingly accepted.

That gap defines the standard this article proposes:

Everything that is decided must be publishable.

Publishable does not mean published. It means the decision can be reconstructed, explained and defended before anyone with a legitimate right to examine it (the board, the auditor, the supervisor and, where appropriate, the market), even if part of the information behind it remains confidential. Publishability is a property of the decision. Publication is a decision about information. Confusing the two is where the problem starts.

When Transparency Arrives Too Late

The industry tends to place transparency at the end of the process. First you decide; then you disclose what is required. Transparency thus ends up filed under compliance, supervisory relations or reputation.

Regulation does not require this way of thinking. Basel Pillar 3 calls for meaningful, comparable and consistent public disclosure to support market discipline. Where it allows proprietary or confidential information to be withheld, it requires banks to state why and to provide more general information instead. Confidentiality is no exemption from explanation: it demands that the confidentiality itself be explained.

The mistake lies in treating disclosure as the point where transparency begins. When that happens, confidentiality starts being used backwards, not to protect information but to avoid explaining how a decision was reached, and the quality of a decision ends up being judged by whether due process was followed.

Approving a decision is not the same as governing it.

The Infrastructure No One Sees

In 2013, when it issued its principles for effective risk data aggregation and risk reporting (BCBS 239), the Basel Committee acknowledged that the crisis that began in 2007 had exposed a serious shortcoming. Many banks were unable to aggregate their risk exposures or identify concentrations completely, quickly and accurately, and this impaired their ability to make timely decisions. The problem came before communication: the information needed to decide could not be assembled at the speed the decision required.

In its 2025 supervisory review, the ECB continues to find shortcomings in the accuracy, integrity, completeness and timeliness of that data. Its May 2024 guide on risk data aggregation and risk reporting insists on the same infrastructure: data lineage, clear ownership and the capacity to produce reliable information whenever management and the supervisor need it.

Traceable data is not enough to explain a decision; without traceability, no explanation can be verified. That is where transparency really begins: in being able to trace a data point from its source to the decision it helped shape. And that is also where alignment is defined. An institution is aligned when what the board receives, what the supervisor examines and what the market learns are versions of the same reasoning.

A decision that needs a different argument for each audience is not governed.

The risk posed by a decision that cannot be reconstructed stays hidden because no one asks while results hold up. The question comes with the inspection, the loss or the crisis, when there is less time to answer it and when those who knew the rationale may no longer be in the room.

The Cost of Not Being Able to Explain

Being unable to explain is paid for in senior management hours. Analyses are redone, assumptions are rebuilt and responses to the supervisor are delayed. Management debates which number is right before it can debate what to do.

There is also an external cost. In a sample of 135 banks across Europe, North America and Australia, Poshakwale and Courtis associated higher levels of disclosure with a lower cost of equity capital, with risk management disclosures carrying the most weight. An ECB working paper on the EU-wide stress tests from 2014 to 2021 sharpens the point: publication provided new information to the market, and banks with weaker results saw lower returns and higher volatility. Transparency does not reward everyone. It allows the market to tell banks apart.

Confidentiality Also Governs

None of this means the answer is to disclose more. Banking handles information whose confidentiality is essential, such as customer data, proprietary information and confidential supervisory information.

The line, therefore, is not between transparency and secrecy, but between legitimate confidentiality and opacity. The first protects information whose disclosure would cause identifiable harm. The second prevents anyone from understanding how a decision was made. Confidentiality itself must also be documented: what is withheld, why, and who decided it.

Confidentiality protects information. It does not protect indefensible decisions.

Decide as if You Had to Explain It Tomorrow

In 2009, the Federal Reserve subjected the 19 largest US bank holding companies to a capital assessment under two scenarios, a baseline and a more adverse one, and sized the capital buffers against the latter. Departing from the usual confidentiality of supervision, it published the methodology and firm-level results. Ten needed to raise roughly US$74.6 billion in capital. By November of that year, nine had done so without drawing on the public capital backstop; the tenth, GMAC, expected to cover its shortfall with Treasury support. The Federal Reserve itself concluded that publication had helped to increase confidence.

In day-to-day governance, the rule applies to everything that is decided; the record, to what is material: anything that commits capital, liquidity or risk appetite, or requires approval by the board or its committees. No material decision should reach approval without answering three questions:

  1. Source: where does each relevant data point come from, and who is accountable for it?
  2. Rationale: which assumptions, rejected alternatives and accepted risks support the decision?
  3. Boundaries: what may each audience know, what is withheld, and why?

The three answers, in writing and before approval, make up the publishability record. The record does not replace the minutes: it captures the reasoning that supports the decision, not the deliberation that preceded it. The aim is not to publish it, but to confirm that the decision could be explained tomorrow without having to rebuild today what no one made clear yesterday.

The record is also the test of alignment: the board summary, the supervisory response and the market communication may differ in detail, but they must all derive from the same data, assumptions and risks. If they do not, the institution does not have a governed decision: it has three stories.

The trade-off is real: approval becomes slower. Two groups will push back. Those who measure their performance by speed of approval will see a brake. Those who benefit from ambiguity will lose room to reinterpret a decision once the outcome becomes inconvenient. That speed is recovered under stress, when the institution no longer needs to reconstruct before it can respond.

The diagnostic can be requested tomorrow: the board randomly draws a fixed number of material decisions from the full register of the past twelve months and asks for them to be reconstructed without notice. The time taken and the versions that surface set the baseline. From then on, two things are tracked across comparable decisions: the time needed to answer the three questions without redoing the analysis, and the discrepancies in data, assumptions or risks between versions. If the time does not fall period after period, or the discrepancies do not disappear, the record has become a box-ticking exercise.

That is the competitive advantage. Not that the market pays more for transparency, but that the institution stops paying for being unable to explain itself.

A decision that cannot be reconstructed is not defended in a crisis: it is exposed by one.
